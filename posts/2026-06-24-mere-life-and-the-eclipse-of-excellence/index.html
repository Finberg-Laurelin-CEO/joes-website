Mere Life and the Eclipse of Excellence

Apollo was the last great thing the American state would attempt for its own sake.

Published 2026-06-24 · Long · Tags politics, philosophy, apollo

“The state comes into existence, originating in the bare needs of life, and continuing in existence for the sake of a good life.”
Aristotle, Politics**

“I can’t pay no doctor bill, but Whitey’s on the moon.”
Gil Scott-Heron, 1970

On July 15, 1969, the day before Apollo 11 left for the Moon, a Baptist minister named Ralph Abernathy walked up to the gates of the Kennedy Space Center behind four mules and two wooden wagons. He had brought about twenty-five poor families with him. A few miles off, the Saturn V stood thirty-six stories tall on its pad. Abernathy had come to protest what he called the country’s distorted sense of national priorities, and to ask why a nation that could send men to the Moon could not feed the children living within sight of the launch towers.

Thomas O. Paine, the head of NASA (no, not that one), came out into the field to meet him. Paine did not argue that the rocket mattered more than the children. He said the opposite. “If it were possible for us not to push that button and solve the problems you are talking about, we would not push that button,” he told Abernathy, and then asked him to hitch his wagon to the rocket. The next morning the button was pushed. Half a billion people watched. By the count of the journalist Charles Fishman, it was the largest non-military effort in the history of the species, drawing on 400,000 workers across some twenty thousand companies.

It was also nearly the end. Three and a half years later the program was over, and no human has stood on the Moon since. The usual explanation is money: Vietnam, inflation, a tightening budget. That explanation is true, and it misses the point. The deeper change was in what Americans would agree to pay for. During the years Apollo flew, the federal government quietly rebuilt itself around a different purpose. It stopped being mainly an instrument for doing great and dangerous things and became an instrument for keeping people alive, insured, and comfortable. Apollo did not lose a fight with the welfare office. It lost its standing in a country that had decided, with a good conscience, that the first duty of government is to relieve suffering, and that everything else has to justify itself against that duty. Once that becomes the rule, a rocket cannot win the argument. Neither can anything else built for glory rather than need.

That is a claim about governance, not about a single budget fight. It has little to do, in the end, with which programs traded places in which year. It is about the standard by which a society judges its highest ambitions: whether politics is only the management of necessity, or whether it also exists to make possible a greatness that cannot be justified in the language of need. That question decides what anyone who hopes to build something on the scale of Apollo should now expect from the state that once built such things.

A budget that stopped adding up

Apollo was enormous. The Planetary Society puts the cost of the program at $25.8 billion in the dollars of the day, around $309 billion now, and at its peak in 1966, three years before the landing, NASA took up about 4.5 percent of the entire federal budget. For most of the decade, three of every five dollars the country spent on space went to the Moon.

Now look at what the same government was doing with the rest of its money. In 1965, at the height of Apollo spending, Congress created Medicare and Medicaid. Over the next ten years the shape of the federal budget inverted. The spending a government is obligated to pay out, mostly Social Security and the new health programs, rose from under 30 percent of federal spending in 1962 to about 45 percent by 1975. The Congressional Budget Office records the mirror image: the discretionary money a government actually chooses how to spend, which includes almost all of its investment in new and ambitious things, fell as a share of the budget as the obligated payments climbed.

This is the real story beneath the budget numbers, and it is not a story about one program beating another in a single year. The country did not cancel the Moon to write a particular welfare check. It rebuilt its finances around transfer and maintenance, and within that new structure a project like Apollo no longer had a place to stand. The Saturn V was the kind of thing the old government did once and at great cost. The check to a retiree or a hospital is the kind of thing the new government does by design, at scale, and more or less forever. When the historian John Logsdon asked the people who ended Apollo why they did it, the answers were budgetary and almost bored. Richard Nixon, an aide reported, did not see the need to go to the Moon six more times. Paine told the president the decisive reason was the austere funding situation. The public, Logsdon writes, seemed indifferent.

The public got what it asked for

It is tempting to read all this as something done to the public by its leaders. The polling says otherwise. The historian Roger Launius, who spent years going through the surveys, found that for most of the 1960s a majority of Americans did not think the Moon landing was worth the cost. Only once, in the days around Apollo 11 itself, did a bare 53 percent say the result justified the expense. A Harris poll two years earlier found 54 percent against the program’s price. Through the decade, space ranked near the top of the list of things people wanted cut.

This matters because it tells you the change is not a policy you can reverse by winning an election. The preference for the doctor’s bill over the rocket was not imposed from above by planners or ideologues. It came from below, it was held with a clear conscience, and it has stayed. A government answerable to that preference will, election after election, choose care over grandeur, because that is what the people who elect it actually want. The retrospective glow around Apollo is a trick of memory. At the time, the country paid for the Moon while looking the other way, and stopped paying for it as soon as the contest with the Soviets was won.

Life, and the good life

To see what was lost, and what was gained, it helps to return to the oldest argument about what governments are for. Aristotle drew a line that the whole tradition after him kept circling back to. A city, he wrote, comes into being “originating in the bare needs of life, and continuing in existence for the sake of a good life”. Survival is where politics starts. It is not where politics is meant to end. Food, safety, and health are the conditions of a decent common life, not its purpose. A community that treats them as the purpose has, by this standard, mistaken the floor for the ceiling.

Mere life is a precise idea, not an insult. The Greeks had two words where we have one. There was ζωή (zoē), the bare fact of being alive, the heartbeat and the metabolism a man shares with a dog or a vine. And there was βίος (bios), a life with a shape and a story, the kind you can give an account of: a soldier’s life, a craftsman’s life, the life of a particular person aimed at particular ends. The first is what you have; the second is what you make of having it. The household and the village, Aristotle said, exist to secure ζωή, the feeding and sheltering and breeding that keep the species alive. The city exists for βίος, the good life pursued in common. Survival was the ground floor of politics, never the whole building. The things a government now spends most of its money on, food and health and shelter and income and the care of the old, were to the Greeks pre-political: necessary, honorable in their place, and beneath the dignity of the public square.

The older world took that ranking for granted and stated it without apology. When Homer’s Achilles is given the choice between a long, safe, forgotten life and a short one whose name will live for ever, he takes the glory, and the poem honors him for it. I will not pretend to stand above that choice. It was the right one. The ethic behind it is frankly aristocratic, and that is no defect in it: excellence is by nature rare, unequal, and bought at a cost most people will never agree to pay, and a society that ranks the comfort of the many over the greatness of the few has not grown more just, only smaller. Necessity is the condition of the higher life, not its purpose. The leisure and order and security that a decent provision buys are worth having for the sake of what they make possible above themselves: strength of body in discipline and bearing, strength of mind in learning and judgment, and the strength that shows in the scale and durability of what a man builds and leaves behind. A civilization that secures the base and then asks for nothing higher has spent its whole inheritance on the rent. For most of recorded history the people who thought seriously about politics assumed that staying alive was the beginning of the subject, not its end, and that the worth of a people was settled by what it did above and beyond keeping itself alive. Apollo belonged to that older measure. It was expensive, dangerous, and useless in any direct sense, and that was the point of it.

The standard goes deeper than the battlefield. In a recent and deliberately unfashionable book, Selective Breeding and the Birth of Philosophy, the political theorist Costin Alamariu argues that philosophy itself, the discovery of nature as a measure standing above mere custom, was an aristocratic achievement. The thought that there is a real order of higher and lower, fixed in the nature of things rather than decreed by the city, came first to men formed by the perception of breeding and inherited quality, men placed to notice that the laws and moralities around them were local and changeable while excellence was not. It is the position Plato hands to Callicles in the Gorgias: that the conventions which level the strong down to the average are a swindle the many work on the few, and that the justice of nature runs the other way. One need not follow that argument to its furthest conclusions to see its bearing here. The standard by which Apollo counts as greatness and a monthly check counts as mere upkeep is a standard of nature in just this sense, a claim that some things are higher than others and that a people is judged by whether it still reaches for them.

The triumph of necessity

The modern state was built on the opposite assumption, and its builders were honest about it. Thomas Hobbes founded political authority on the one thing every person wants without being taught to want it: not to die. The sovereign exists to spare men “continual fear, and danger of violent death”, and a government that keeps you alive has met its basic test. Hobbes made that choice deliberately. Men quarrel without end about what the good life is, and they kill one another over the answer, but they agree about the worst thing, a violent death, and a state founded on avoiding the worst thing is steadier than one founded on a contested picture of the best. It was a brilliant lowering of the aim. Politics would no longer try to make men excellent. It would keep them alive and leave them to seek their own comfort in peace. Almost everything humane and disenchanted about the modern state is folded into that single move.

Two centuries later Benjamin Constant described where it led. The ancients, he wrote, found liberty in sharing power; the moderns find it in “the enjoyment of security in private pleasures”. The citizen of a small Greek city had governed it directly, sat in its assembly, judged its trials, and gave up his private life almost entirely to the community in return. The modern man has the opposite bargain. He hands the running of the state to representatives and asks only to be left alone: to trade, to travel, to worship or not, to enjoy his own house and his own opinions without interference. Liberty had meant a share in power. Now it meant safety in private things. Constant thought the trade was mostly worth making, and warned only that a people absorbed in private pleasure might let its public freedom slip away without noticing it go.

Tocqueville saw where it pointed. He imagined a future power that would be gentle rather than cruel, “an immense and tutelary power” that keeps its citizens in perpetual childhood, that provides for their security, foresees and supplies their needs, and asks only that they stop trying to govern themselves. Such a power, he wrote, does not tyrannize; “it compresses, enervates, extinguishes, and stupefies a people, till each nation is reduced to be nothing better than a flock of timid and industrious animals, of which the government is the shepherd.” He wrote that in 1840, before anything we would call a welfare state existed. It reads like a description of one.

What Tocqueville feared, the twentieth century built, and Hannah Arendt gave it its sharpest description. She divided human activity into three kinds. There is labor, the unending work of the body to keep itself going, the eating and cleaning and earning and healing that are never finished, because the moment they are done they begin again. There is work, the making of durable things, the building of a world of objects that outlasts the maker. And there is action, the public deed done in front of others, the word and act for which a person is remembered, the only activity through which, she thought, a human being shows who he actually is. In the older world a free man was one who had mastered the necessities of mere life and could then enter the public square to do something worth remembering. Necessity was the precondition of freedom, and it was kept firmly in its place, inside the household, out of public view.

The modern age, in Arendt’s account, has let labor swallow the other two. The concerns of the body, of staying fed and healthy and secure, have climbed out of the private household where the Greeks confined them and taken over the public realm. She called it the rise of the social: the moment when the business of the life process itself becomes the main thing public power is for. A modern government counts its population, tracks its diseases, insures its old age, screens its blood, regulates its risks, and measures its own success in years of life added. It is very good at all of this, and getting better. What it has quietly given up is the older thing, the thing Arendt called action. Apollo was action in its purest form: a deed performed in the open, at enormous risk, for the memory of it. A monthly benefit payment is labor, humane and necessary and without end. A state organized around the second has little room left for the first.

What the older critics rarely admit, and what an honest version of this argument has to, is that the maintenance state is good. A government that bites back at the rat in the tenement, the rat in Gil Scott-Heron’s poem that bit his sister Nell while Whitey was on the Moon, is doing something humane and worth doing. The maintenance state did not win because people were tricked, and it did not win because they were cowards. It won because relieving suffering is a real good, and because almost everyone, offered the choice between the rocket and the doctor, will choose the doctor. There is nothing cowardly in that, and an argument that says otherwise is not worth making.

The committee of investigation

The trouble starts with everything the doctor cannot justify. A government that earns its legitimacy by relieving suffering has no ready answer for a project that relieves no one, and over time it stops attempting such projects at all. Carl Schmitt, writing between the wars, described the political type that this kind of society produces. He called the liberal middle class a “discussing class”, a group that answers every hard question by forming a committee. Faced with the demand to choose between Christ and Barabbas, he wrote, it responds with a proposal to adjourn or to appoint a commission of investigation. Schmitt’s deeper claim was about where such a society ends up. A world organized wholly around safety and commerce, he warned, is not really a community at all. It is closer to an arrangement among tenants in a tenement house, or passengers traveling on the same bus: people who share a roof and a route and nothing else worth sacrificing for.

Such a society has a hard time with a project like Apollo, and the reason is structural. Once the only fully legitimate political purpose is the relief of human suffering, every other purpose has to be translated into that language or stand accused. Schmitt saw the move coming and put it with deliberate edge: whoever invokes humanity, he wrote, wants to cheat. He meant that an appeal to suffering humanity at large is the most powerful weapon there is against any particular project that serves something other than need. A rocket has no answer to a sick child, because the rocket was never about need in the first place. Apollo was about prestige, daring, and the wish of one nation to do a great thing because it could.

Nietzsche, half a century before Schmitt, had sketched the man who would find such a wish unintelligible. He called him the last man, the one who has “discovered happiness,” who keeps warm, avoids risk, takes his small pleasures, and blinks. Nietzsche’s deeper worry was not the comfort itself but the morality that grows up around it. A code built on safety and pity, he argued, does not merely shelter the weak; in time it teaches a whole people to distrust the dangerous, the extravagant, and the high, to treat ambition as a disorder and risk as something close to a sin. A project that asks men to gamble their lives for a flag and a footprint is exactly the kind of thing such a morality learns to find faintly embarrassing. The last man is not a monster. He is pleasant, healthy, and reasonable. He has simply lost the capacity to want anything beyond his own comfort, and he cannot see why a sane person would spend a fortune to leave footprints on a dead world when there are bills to pay. The maintenance state is the last man’s government, and it governs well by his lights. Christopher Caldwell has traced how, since the 1960s, this instinct hardened into a settled politics of grievance and redistribution, in which more and more of public life comes to be judged by what it redresses rather than by what it achieves.

The tribunal of suffering

The strongest case against everything I have said was made in that field outside the launch gates, and it deserves an answer rather than a sneer. In 1969 the poverty rate among Black Americans was 31 percent. The families who marched on the space center were not inventing their complaints. And the objection runs deeper than any quarrel over one year’s budget. Pressed to its limit it becomes a real question, perhaps the hardest this argument has to meet: if a civilization can buy its greatness only by spending the lives of the helpless, is the greatness worth anything at all? A monument raised on the bones of the men who quarried it, a palace that runs on slaves, a splendor paid for in deliberate starvation: these are not triumphs with a footnote. They are crimes with good lighting. The honest friend of excellence should be the first to say so, because excellence and atrocity are not the same thing, and a people that can no longer tell them apart has already failed at the only judgment that finally matters.

That question, taken seriously, does not convict Apollo. It convicts something else, and the critique at the gates survives only by quietly confusing the two. There is a difference between greatness built by sacrificing the helpless and greatness pursued while the helpless suffer, and the difference is the whole moral universe. Nobody starved so that the Saturn V could fly. The poor of 1969 were exactly as poor on the morning of the launch as they had been the night before. The money not spent on them was not spent on them, but they were never its raw material, and to decline to pour every public dollar into the relief of need is not to feed the needy into the engine. The opportunity cost of a great work is not a human sacrifice. Treating the two as one thing is the central sleight of hand of the argument from suffering. Apollo asked nothing of the rat-bitten child in the poem except that her country lift its eyes from her for a few years. That is a real cost. It is not a corpse.

Push the objection the other way and it swallows everything. If no great thing may be attempted while anyone still suffers, then almost nothing any civilization ever built was permitted. The cathedral should have been a poorhouse. The university should have been a granary. The observatory, the concert hall, the polar expedition, the long centuries of research that returned nothing for generations: each of them took bread from someone’s mouth, in the only sense that ever applies, which is that the money could always have gone to bread instead. A standard that condemns the Moon condemns all of it. What presents itself as compassion turns out to be a rule under which nothing may be reached for until everything has first been repaired, which is to say never. The man who presses it has not chosen the poor over the rocket. He has chosen a civilization that builds nothing, and then dressed the choice up as kindness.

The same instinct is remaking the institutions that were built to produce excellence in the first place. An elite university was never a neutral machine for sorting talent. It is a body for forming and continuing an elite, for transmitting standards and binding a class together across generations. Govern it by the logic of redress and you get admissions that turn on race, which abandon any settled measure of excellence while speaking fluently the language of fairness, and which require treating like applicants unlike on the ground of ancestry. Govern it by pure procedure and you get a sorting machine that has forgotten what it was for. Legacy preference, whatever else can be said against it, is at least honest about the purpose of the place. The leveling instinct cannot tell a standard from a privilege, and so in the end it dissolves both.

Greatness leaves the state

None of this means the modern state is impotent. The same government that walked away from the Moon seeded the early internet, mapped the human genome, and paid for the research behind half the objects in your pocket. But the list has a shape. The maintenance state will reliably do great things only when they can be defended as forms of care: public health, national security, insurance against catastrophe. It will not, as a standing matter, tax a comfortable and reluctant public for glory, for prestige, or for a frontier that pays no dividend anyone can feel. That is not a defect to be cured by better leadership. It is the resting state of a government organized around mere life, and the polls of 1967 will be the polls of 2067. Waiting for the state to recover its nerve is a category error. The nerve is gone because the purpose changed, and the purpose changed because, on the whole, the public wanted it to.

So the work of civilizational ambition has been moving out of the state and into other hands, and it will keep moving. The astronauts NASA now sends to orbit ride on a capsule built and flown by a private company, the first private spacecraft to carry people to the station. The most powerful rocket ever built lifts off from a private launch site in south Texas, not from a government program. Whether those firms or their owners are admirable is beside the question. What matters is where the capacity for great and risky undertakings now lives. It has left the agency and gone to the company, the laboratory, and the foundation, because those are the few places that can still commit to an aim a majority would never vote to fund.

I have a direct stake in this, which I should name. I run a company trying to build fusion energy, one of roughly fifty such firms that have together raised close to ten billion dollars in private money, chasing a result that government laboratories have pursued for seventy years without reaching it. I am, in other words, exactly the kind of person who profits from the argument I am making here, and you should weigh it with that in mind. But the pattern is larger than any one industry. The hard, expensive, non-obvious frontiers, the ones that look like Apollo rather than like a transfer payment, are increasingly opened by private capital and small mission-driven institutions, precisely because they no longer have to win a referendum against the doctor’s bill.

I am not asking anyone to defund the clinic and buy a rocket. That argument is lost, and it deserved to lose. A government answerable to a comfortable majority will choose the clinic every time, and no election will reverse it. The division that matters is not the one between left and right, both of which now ask first what benefit accrues at once, to whom, and at what distributive cost. It is the division between a politics that takes the management of necessity as the whole of its business and a politics that still holds that a people is judged by what it dares above necessity. The first will always command the votes. The second now has to be built by the few who still believe it, in the firms and laboratories and endowments that answer to a founder rather than to a poll. Abernathy asked what a country owes the people living in the shadow of its towers. The honest answer is a great deal, and not everything, and never the tower itself. Any civilization can keep its people alive. A civilization earns the name when it makes, now and then, something that justifies their being alive at all. The button Paine pushed in 1969 was the last of its kind a public would agree to pay for. The next one will be privately owned, and it should be.
